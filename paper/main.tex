\begin{abstract}
Probing studies of latent reasoning read a model's state where the answer begins, and take a successful probe as evidence that an intermediate value was computed and a failed one as evidence that it was not. The second fails even for a digit copied from the question, and the first is ambiguous: using prompts that differ in a single token, we show that such a digit is present at that position in two looped and two standard language models, while a probe that sees one prompt at a time reads it at no more than 0.52 even after 45,000 training questions, and every token we varied leaves such a trace, so a probe for a computed value may be reading the inputs. We give a protocol that tests a candidate computed value against copies of the inputs, against generic mixing of them with an operation swap whose bar is set on synthetic mixtures, and for use with a clamp that controls for the copy. We check each test where a value is certainly computed: the clamp detects values the model is about to write or reads four tokens later, and, in small transformers trained from scratch, an unwritten value used eight tokens later; our first, accuracy-based swap failed its positive control, so we replaced it with a version based on shares of variance that passed two fresh controls before we applied it, though in the small transformers it fires wherever the value is present, used or not, and in one of 270 null worlds. In the looped Ouro-1.4B the protocol finds the unwritten intermediate's sign following the operation beyond copies, the answer, the carry and borrow, and generic mixing of the operands, though narrowly and as a small part of the state, which cannot be told apart from the answer with a copied number added back, and no running total shows before that number is read; its use at the position tested is unresolved: a clamp moves the answer in 4\% of pairs, against 0.3\% for a random one. For a published claim that language models compute sub-answers only while writing a chain of thought, the protocol cannot tell: its operation swap is unclear before the chain, as it is three tokens before a value is written, and the sub-answer's own directions barely move the output, while a test against copies alone and a clamp that leaves the copy in place would both have suggested an early sub-answer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Looped transformers apply one block of layers repeatedly \citep{geiping2025,zhu2025ouro}, and one hope is that the extra loops work as a hidden chain of thought; a related question is whether standard models compute sub-answers before they write a chain at all \citep{kudo2026}. Accuracy alone cannot settle either. Answering directly after the question, Ouro-1.4B's GSM8K accuracy rises from 6.5\% after one loop to 23\% after four, but almost all of the gain comes at loop 2, where Ouro also improves on benchmarks that involve no arithmetic (Appendix~\ref{app:acc}). To see whether intermediate values are computed, one has to look inside.

Probing studies of latent reasoning read the state at the position where the answer begins, which we call the summary position, and take a successful probe as evidence that a value was computed and a failed one as evidence that it was not \citep{lu2025,yu2024,kudo2026}. The second fails even for the simplest value there is, a digit copied from the question, and the first is ambiguous for the same reason. Our main contribution is a protocol for computed values whose tests are each checked where the answer is known:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The problem} (Section~\ref{sec:probes}). Minimal pairs, prompts that differ in a single token, show the copied digit at the last prompt token of two looped models (Ouro-1.4B and 2.6B) and two standard ones (Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B), 0.79--0.88 on average in three models and 0.92--0.97 across Ouro-1.4B's states. A probe that sees one prompt at a time, as probing studies use, reads it at 0.12--0.27 with tens of training templates and at most 0.52 after 45,000 training questions. Every token we varied leaves such a trace, so a probe that reads a ``computed'' value may be reading the inputs.
\item \textbf{The protocol} (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}). A candidate value is tested against copies of the inputs, against generic mixing with an operation swap whose bar is set on calibrated synthetic mixtures, and for use with a clamp that controls for the copy. Each step rejects something that a weaker test accepts in our data (Table~\ref{tab:steps}).
\item \textbf{Validation} (Section~\ref{sec:control}). In pre-registered positive controls the copy-controlled clamp detects a value the model is about to write (+0.61 over a random clamp) and one it reads four tokens after writing it (+0.81). Our first, accuracy-based operation swap failed its control, because a code of the operand pair makes every function of the pair readable; a swap based on shares of variance (the share swap), frozen before testing, passed two fresh controls in two model families, with no false positive in 420 null worlds (later, 1 in 270 on another). In small transformers trained from scratch the clamp also detects an unwritten value used eight tokens later, while the share swap fires wherever that value is present.
\item \textbf{Two applications.} In Ouro-1.4B (Section~\ref{sec:ouro}) the unwritten intermediate $a+b$ is readable just before the answer's last digit, and its sign follows the operation beyond the copies, the answer, the carry and borrow, and a bar set by generic mixing on its own design, though narrowly: on plus problems the sum leads the difference by 0.001 of the variance, its interval just above zero. The effect is small (under 1\% of the remaining variance), it cannot be told apart from the answer with $c$ added back, and whether it is used is unresolved: clamping it moves the written digit in 4\% of pairs (random 0.3\%), and the pre-registered comparison proved weak. For the claim of \citet{kudo2026} that sub-answers are computed only while the chain is written (Section~\ref{sec:kudo}), the protocol cannot tell, with or without a chain: the share swap is UNCLEAR before the chain, as in a control three tokens before a value is written, and $b$'s own directions change the output in at most 3\% of pairs, a clamp with shown power only with a chain; weaker tests would have suggested an early sub-answer.
\end{itemize}
Most studies were pre-registered, and the text marks the analyses that were not; Appendix~\ref{app:status} gives the status of every analysis.

\section{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

Ouro-1.4B and 2.6B \citep{zhu2025ouro} run their layers up to four times; we read the state in the middle of loop 1 and at the end of each loop, in fp32 (on check prompts our forward pass matches Ouro's own, with a largest logit difference of 0). Qwen2.5-1.5B and 7B \citep{qwen2025} are standard transformers, read after layers 7, 14, 21 and 28 (and 4 in the 1.5B). All four give each digit its own token. Every prompt in Sections~\ref{sec:probes} and~\ref{sec:ouro} starts with the first four GSM8K training problems \citep{cobbe2021}, each followed directly by its final answer, then the test question and ``Answer:''.

The \emph{standard probe} standardizes the state, projects it onto 256 principal components fitted on the training folds, and fits a multinomial logistic regression whose regularization is chosen by inner cross-validation. It is scored on 5 held-out folds grouped by template, so it must generalize to unseen kinds of problem. The \emph{minimal-pair probe} is the same probe on states from which each family's mean has been subtracted. Labels are units digits. We probe P0, the last prompt token, and, in Section~\ref{sec:ouro}, P1: with the gold two-digit answer fed in, the tens-digit token, after which the units digit is predicted.

\section{Why a probe at the summary position is ambiguous}\label{sec:probes}

\paragraph{A copied digit is present but hard to read.}\label{sec:presence} At the last prompt token a standard probe does not read a digit copied from the question. In a pre-registered study its sanity check, the last digit of the largest number in the question, scored 40.8\%, exactly the rate of always guessing the most common digit; by position, a copied digit is read perfectly on its own token and at chance from three tokens on (Appendix~\ref{sec:mp}). We therefore took one question per template and one of its multi-digit numbers, and made ten copies that differ only in that number's last digit: 137 families from 77 distinct questions. The copies are identical token for token except one, so any difference in a later state is caused by that digit. Counterfactual minimal pairs are a standard interpretability control \citep{stolfo2023,vig2020investigating}; subtracting a family's mean removes what the family shares, the contrast that difference-in-means probing uses to find a direction \citep{marks2023}.

The minimal-pair probe reads the digit on templates it never saw, 0.79--0.88 on average in three models and 0.92--0.97 across Ouro-1.4B's states, while the standard probe reads it at 0.12--0.27 (Table~\ref{tab:presence}, Figure~\ref{fig:readout}). A structured decoder, the best single-prompt decoder we could build (a mass-mean probe from the minimal-pair digit means with a shrunk covariance of the training prompts), does no better (Appendix~\ref{sec:many}). The code is shared across questions: in Ouro-1.4B a probe trained on needed numbers reads a number in an added, irrelevant sentence at 0.78--0.97, and a minimal-pair probe of a colour word in that sentence reads it at 0.99--1.00. It is not a magnitude direction \citep{zhuf2024}, since the probe's errors are no closer to the true digit than random guesses, which fits per-digit codes \citep{levy2025}. The minimal-pair probe needs all ten variants of a question, so it tests presence; it is not a decoder for a new prompt.

\paragraph{More questions do not close the gap.}\label{sec:more} In pre-registered studies we gave each model one prompt for each of 6,249 GSM8K training questions, with the units digit of the last number of two or more digits set at random, held out a fixed 1,000, and trained probes on nested subsets of the rest (Figure~\ref{fig:curve}); for the larger models we added 40,000 Orca-Math questions \citep{mitra2024orcamath}, after removing any that reword a test question. At 5,249 training questions the best single-prompt probe reads 0.19 in Qwen2.5-1.5B and 0.36 in Ouro-1.4B; at 45,249 it reads 0.31 in Ouro-2.6B and 0.52 at Qwen2.5-7B's last layer (at most 0.29 at its other layers), against 0.79--0.96 for minimal pairs on the same test questions. The last doubling adds under 0.01 on the GSM8K test questions but still 0.014--0.018 on Orca-Math ones, so data closer to the test questions may read more. The claim is that presence and single-prompt readability come apart by a wide margin at the data sizes tested, not that the digit cannot be read.

\paragraph{Why it is hard to read.}\label{sec:small} The digit's directions are no more crowded than those of a random word swapped in for it or of noise added to its embedding; its change is simply small, and the standard probe reads it about as poorly as the noise (pre-registered; Appendix~\ref{sec:swamp}).

\paragraph{Both kinds of result are ambiguous.} A failed probe at a summary position is weak evidence that a value is absent: a digit that is demonstrably present reads below 0.30 in most states. This concerns probes that must generalize to new questions; on prompts that differ only in their numbers, such as bare additions, a standard probe reads both operands at 0.96--1.00 (Appendix~\ref{sec:mp}). A successful probe is weak evidence of computation: every token we varied, an irrelevant number and a colour word included, leaves a trace at the same position, so a probe that reads a ``computed'' value may be reading the inputs it is a function of. In small transformers without varied context a computed value shows no such gap (post hoc, Appendix~\ref{app:v230}); computed values in language models are untested. The protocol addresses the second problem.

\section{A protocol for computed values}\label{sec:protocol}

Within a minimal-pair family one digit varies, so any computed value is a fixed function of it. The protocol therefore varies the operands together in factorial families, with everything else fixed, and asks five questions of a candidate value such as $(a+b) \bmod 5$:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Is it more than copies?} If the state holds $f(a)+g(b)$, a linear probe on it does no better than the same probe on one-hot codes of the digits, since any function of a digit is linear in its one-hot code. We call that probe's accuracy the \emph{copy ceiling}, a conditional probe with the inputs as its baseline \citep{hewitt2021conditional}, and score every label as its excess over its own ceiling. A prime modulus keeps other combinations from sharing the target's parity. Excess shows only that the operands were COMBINED.
\item \textbf{Does it follow the operation?} A generic mixture makes many combinations readable, $a+b$ and $a-b$ alike. Problems therefore come in twin families that differ only in the operation. After regressing the copies (and, where it confounds, the answer) out of the family-centred states, we take the share of the remaining variance that each label's class means explain on held-out families. With $D_+$ the share of $a+b$ minus that of $a-b$ on plus problems, and $D_-$ the reverse on minus problems, $I = (D_+ + D_-)/2$ cancels any fixed preference for one sign. FOLLOWS requires both $D$s positive, $I$'s interval above zero, and $I$ at least 0.30 of $N$, the needed label's share ($a+b$'s on plus problems and $a-b$'s on minus ones, averaged); DOES-NOT-FOLLOW, $I$'s upper bound below $0.10N$; otherwise UNCLEAR. We call this the share swap. An earlier version compared probe accuracies instead (the accuracy swap); it failed its positive control (Section~\ref{sec:control}).
\item \textbf{Is it more than mixing?} Synthetic states hold copies of the inputs and one generic nonlinear mixture (products of random codes for pairs of inputs, same-frequency products of periodic digit features, a random network, or a random code for each operand pair, with or without the operation), but no term for the candidate. The bar of 0.30 is 1.5 times the largest ratio among 420 such worlds of varied mixing strength (0.19), rounded up; none of them passes it, nor any of 420 fresh ones (Section~\ref{sec:control}).
\item \textbf{Does it rank first?} (Descriptive; not part of the decision.) Compare the target with every combination of the inputs, since periodic number features \citep{zhou2024fourier,kantamneni2025trigonometry} make some combinations easier to read than others.
\item \textbf{Is it used?} Replace the state's component in the candidate's subspace (spanned by family-centred class means, fitted on other families) with that of a partner problem, and read the output. Replacing the whole state is the positive control, and a random subspace the negative one. The subspace is projected off the copy's (and, before an answer, the answer's), since otherwise a clamp can act through the copied digit. USED needs a gain of at least 0.10 over the random clamp with the interval above zero; NOT-USED, an upper bound below 0.02.
\end{enumerate}
Results between a step's bars are UNCLEAR, and a test whose accuracy gate or positive control fails is UNSCORABLE. A computed value needs COMBINED and FOLLOWS, and a used one USED as well. Each step rejects something a weaker version accepts in this paper's data (Table~\ref{tab:steps}); Section~\ref{sec:control} tests each step where the answer is known.

\section{Validating the protocol}\label{sec:control}

A protocol that never says yes would tell us little, so pre-registered studies tested it where a computed value is certainly used. All but the last use the two-step problems of \citet{kudo2026}, $a = k \pm b$ and $b = d_1 \pm d_2$ with single-digit values, in a short chain format in which the model writes $b$ and then $a$ (``$b = 5$, $a = 6$''); Qwen2.5-7B writes $b$ correctly in 91\% of 12,000 prompts. The clamp controls were run after the applications in Sections~\ref{sec:ouro} and~\ref{sec:kudo} had found no USED value; the share swap was validated before it was applied (Table~\ref{tab:control}).

\paragraph{The clamp.} One token before $b$ is written, replacing $b$'s directions, with the copy projected out, makes the model write the partner's $b$ in 61\% of pairs, against none for a random subspace (USED). At $b$'s written digit, which the model reads four tokens later to compute $a = k \pm b$, it changes $a$ in 81\% (USED; 63\% where $a = 0 + b$ copies it). Both results are close to guaranteed, since $b$ is the next token or the token itself. At the end of $b$'s equation, a clamp of the copied digit's directions moves the written $b$ six tokens later in 96\% of pairs. Power for a value computed but never written is shown only in the small models below.

\paragraph{The operation swap.} The accuracy-based swap failed: one token before $b$ is written, the operands are combined (+0.25) but $I = +0.017$, below its bar, so the protocol did not call $b$ computed. In an exploratory diagnosis the cause was saturation: there, four random functions of the operand pair read at 0.99--1.00, as $a+b$ and $a-b$ do, so accuracy has no room, while the share of residual variance still separates them. We therefore froze the share swap (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), set its bar from 420 synthetic mixing worlds, and judged it only on controls it had never seen: on new problems in Qwen2.5-7B where $a = k \pm b$, and on $b$ in Mistral-7B, it gives FOLLOWS one token before the value is written, and in 420 fresh null worlds it never does. A Qwen2.5-1.5B control failed its accuracy gate (0.78, below 0.80; unscorable) and was replaced by Mistral (after Qwen2.5-3B failed its accuracy pilot) after that result was known, with the statistic already frozen. Three tokens before $b$ is written, and earlier, the same prompts are UNCLEAR and prefer the difference on both kinds of problem ($D_+ < 0$; $I/N$ 0.61 three tokens before), the pattern of the Kudo positions in Section~\ref{sec:kudo}; so this pattern does not show that a value is absent.

\paragraph{An unwritten value at a distance.} For a value that is computed, used and never written, we trained 6-layer transformers from scratch (5 seeds) on ``$x \circ z$ ; [6 fillers] $w$ ='' with answer $T[x \circ z] + w$ (mod 23), $T$ a fixed permutation, and negative controls on $T_1[x] + T_2[z] \circ w$, which never need $s = x \circ z$. Distributed alignment search \citep{geiger2024finding}, run before any protocol statistic, places $s$ itself (not the operands) at ``;'', eight tokens before the answer, and at most weakly elsewhere (Appendix~\ref{app:v230}). In the pre-registered test the clamp is USED there in all five models (+0.60 to +0.98 over a random subspace; also with no fillers), and not in any negative control or where $s$ is absent. The share swap is FOLLOWS there in all five, but also at every other position, where nothing uses $s$ but a probe reads it beyond the copies (post hoc), and in one of 270 null worlds, at a tiny needed share, so by its pre-registered rule it is not specific. Here a FOLLOWS marks presence, and only the clamp tests use.

\section{Application: an unwritten intermediate in a looped model}\label{sec:ouro}

Studies 1 and 2 used two-step word problems whose answer is $a+b-c$, so the intermediate $a+b$ is never written (``Maya has 30 stickers. Her friend gives her 30 more stickers. She then uses 13 stickers on a card. How many stickers does Maya have left?''), 7,200 problems from 12 templates each. At P1 after loop 4, $a+b$ reads +0.21 (Study 1, mod 10) and +0.22 (Study 2, mod 5) above its copy ceiling, and better than its sign-flipped twin $a-b$ on every one of the 24 templates (post hoc); at P0, before the answer starts, it is not readable. Study 1 missed its pre-registered bar and Study 2's bar was set after it (Appendix~\ref{app:ouro}). Every problem needed $a+b$, so a fixed preference for sums would also explain these results. Study 3 swaps the operation.

\paragraph{Study 3: the operation swap.} Its 12 new templates come in two versions that differ only in the first step's phrase: ``gave her $B$ more stickers'' gives $a+b-c$, and ``took $B$ of her stickers'' gives $a-b-c$. All gates passed (answer-only accuracy 0.67 and 0.77). The accuracy-based swap gives FOLLOWS ($I = +0.157$, Table~\ref{tab:ouro}), but that swap later failed its positive control. Re-scored with the validated share swap (pre-registered after its validation), P1 is still FOLLOWS, with the copies of $a$, $b$, $c$ and the answer's digit regressed out: $a+b$ explains 0.006 of the remaining variance and $a-b$ 0.004 on plus problems, and 0.001 and 0.008 on minus problems, against 0.001 for random functions of the operands on average ($I/N = 0.63$, bar 0.30). P1 is the answer's tens digit, which the carry and borrow fix within a family; with them regressed too (pre-registered) it stays FOLLOWS ($I/N = 0.57$; the sum's lead on plus problems falls to 0.001), against a bar of 0.25 from null worlds rebuilt on this design down to Ouro's signal level (none of 270 passes). P0, before the answer starts, is UNCLEAR. The effect is small: under 1\% of the remaining variance, against 11--15\% where a value is about to be written (Section~\ref{sec:control}). No probe label can separate ``$a+b$ was formed and the answer computed from it'' from ``the answer was formed and $c$ added back'', since on units digits they are the same function. Nor does training order in 18 small transformers trained from scratch (Appendix~\ref{sec:v220}). Where $c$ is not yet read, at the end of the sentence that gives $b$, the share swap is UNCLEAR, though it would likely see a running total as strong as P1's signal, and even the whole state there leaves the answer unchanged (pre-registered; Appendix~\ref{sec:study3}).

\paragraph{Is it used?} For two plus problems from the same family whose units digits of $a+b$ differ but share their parity, we replace $x$'s component at P1 with $x'$'s and read the digit Ouro writes. Patching the answer's subspace after loop 3 moves it to $x'$'s digit in 90\% of pairs; patching $a+b$'s does so in 1.7\%, less often even than $a-b$'s (3.2\%), so the label is NOT-USED (Table~\ref{tab:ouro}). An intact answer could override a weak $a+b$ that had been used to form it, so a further pre-registered study clamped the component at the ends of loops 1, 2 and 3, before the answer forms. Clamping the answer moves the written digit in 91\% of pairs, and clamping $a+b$ in about 4\%, less often than $a-b$: NOT-USED again, also for pairs with equal answers, in which an $a+b$ clamp cannot carry the partner's answer. These interventions predate the copy projection, without which a clamp can also act through the copies. In the small models of Section~\ref{sec:control}, with its fitting data degraded to Ouro's signal level (share 0.005; $a+b$ about 0.008--0.010) and items per dimension, a clamp of this form still moves the answer in 44--98\% of pairs (random 1--12\%; USED in 5 of 5, pre-registered; Appendix~\ref{app:v230}). Ouro's rule, comparing with $a-b$'s subspace, is weak there. Against random alone (post hoc), the loop-3 patch and the clamp are slightly above chance ($+0.017$ [0.008, 0.027], $+0.039$ [0.027, 0.051]), UNCLEAR by the rule, so at P1 the value is readable and its use unresolved.

\paragraph{With a chain of thought.} Their probe's result is reproduced, at a higher level: before the chain it reads $b$ at 0.64 (their 50.5\%) and $a$ at 0.32 (17.9\%). The protocol shows what that readability is (Table~\ref{tab:kudo}). The operands are combined beyond the copy ceiling at both positions (+0.12 to +0.38), but the share swap does not find it following the operation: it is UNCLEAR at both, with the difference preferred on plus and minus problems alike (on plus problems random functions of the operands explain as much). A small part does follow the operation ($I$'s interval is clear of zero), but it is faint: in the short chain, about a fifth of $b$'s code one token before $b$ is written ($I = 0.018$ against 0.087). Nor does the chain use such a value. In their format the chain restates $b$'s equation, and replacing the whole state at either position from layer 12 on never changed the written $b$ in a 40-pair check (not pre-registered). In a short format without the restated equation (``$b = 5$, $a = 6$''), replacing the state at the end of $b$'s equation changes the written $b$ in 96\% of pairs, and so does replacing only the copied digit's directions (96\%), while $b$'s directions with the copy projected out do so in 2\% (+0.021 [0.011, 0.033] over the random clamp; UNCLEAR by the clamp's rule; descriptive, since no position was FOLLOWS).

\paragraph{Without a chain.} Answering directly (their Implicit setting), Qwen2.5-7B is right on 69\% of random problems like theirs (their 77.8\%; 76\% on our families). Their probe reads $b$ at 0.74 just before the answer (theirs 69.1\%). Here the answer is itself a confound, since within a family it fixes $b$, so the share swap regresses the answer out too and the clamp projects $b$'s directions off both. The operands are again combined at every position (+0.14 to +0.38), but with the answer regressed out as well, the share swap is UNCLEAR at every position, with the same preference for the difference and a small part that follows the operation. Replacing the whole state changes the answer to the partner's in 85--86\% of pairs at the two scorable positions (the last prompt token is unscorable). At the end of $b$'s equation the copied digit's directions alone do so in 83\%; just before the answer, the answer's directions do so in 46\%; $b$'s directions with the copy and the answer projected out, in 1\% (NOT-USED) and 3\%. The 3\% is above the random clamp ($+0.025$ [0.014, 0.039]; UNCLEAR, descriptive). The projections keep only 35--54\% of $b$'s directions, too few for the power the clamp has shown (below), so these results say little. Within a family the answer fixes $b$, so this design cannot separate the two either. Their sentence about failures is not contradicted by a check within each pair of operations (post hoc, Appendix~\ref{app:kudo}).

\paragraph{What weaker tests would have said.} By its pre-registered rules the protocol cannot tell whether $b$ is held before the chain, in either setting. No position passes the share swap (all UNCLEAR); at three of the five, $I/N$ is above the bar (0.39--0.59) and $I$ (0.006--0.020) larger than in Ouro (0.003--0.004), and only the sign of $D_+$ keeps them from FOLLOWS. The interventions find little: $b$'s own directions change the written $b$ or answer in 0--3\% of pairs (descriptive). With a chain they keep 75--85\% of $b$'s directions, more than a clamp that in small models moved the answer in 23\% or more of pairs for a fainter used value (here $+0.02$); without a chain, 35--54\%, where it missed one (post hoc; Appendix~\ref{app:v230}). Two weaker tests would have pointed the other way: the operands are combined well beyond the copy ceiling at every position, and a clamp of $b$'s subspace that leaves the copy in place moves the written $b$ in 30\% of pairs and the answer in 28--35\%; projecting out the copy (and, without a chain, the answer) removes almost all of that effect.

\section{Recommendations}\label{sec:recs}

We suggest six practices: test presence with minimal pairs before reading a failed probe as absence; score a candidate against a copy ceiling that includes every input, and the answer and the carry where they fix the token read; swap the operation, and judge the swap on shares of variance against a bar set by synthetic mixtures on the study's own design; do not read an UNCLEAR swap far from where a value is written as absence; read a FOLLOWS as presence, not use; and project the copy out before clamping, with a positive control at the same distance, signal level and share kept.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:lim}

The probing results rest on four models, and the computation studies on Ouro-1.4B and Qwen2.5-7B, each with one kind of two-step arithmetic read where answers begin. In Ouro, Study 2's bar was set after Study 1's near-miss, several comparisons were run afterwards on saved states, and the signal cannot be told apart from the answer with $c$ added back (Appendix~\ref{app:status}). The swap's bar rests on five kinds of synthetic mixing; real states may mix differently. The interventions test a few positions and subspaces spanned by class means: a value held elsewhere and read through attention, or in a code outside those subspaces, would be missed. In a pretrained model the clamp's power is shown only for written values read at most six tokens later, in Qwen2.5-7B; for an unwritten value at a distance, only in small models trained from scratch, at Ouro's signal level only by degrading the clamp's fitting data, and for the form used for \citet{kudo2026} only when the projection keeps 55\% or more of the value, which its clamps do with a chain (75--85\%) but not without (35--54\%). The share swap was validated in two pretrained models and two steps of one task, with one control replaced after the first failed its accuracy gate, and in the small models it was not specific; Ouro, where it gives the paper's one FOLLOWS, is not among them. The re-test of \citet{kudo2026} uses one of their nine models and one task level.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Looped models and latent reasoning.} The answer-only accuracy of the looped Huginn \citep{geiping2025} stays flat across recurrence counts, and a logit lens shows no intermediate values \citep{lu2025}; an unreviewed repository reports the same for Ouro-1.4B-Thinking \citep{mildrgb2026}, \citet{kirin2026} probes Ouro for whether it will answer correctly. Ouro's state is re-derived within each loop \citep{wangreid2026,huang2026partii}, and in models pretrained from scratch at equal depth looping brings no reasoning advantage \citep{schwethelm2026a}. In theory a looped transformer can simulate $T$ chain-of-thought steps in $T$ loops \citep{saunshi2025reasoning}, and models trained to drop written steps can compute intermediates internally \citep{deng2024explicit,hao2025training}, though implicit multi-step arithmetic in models trained from scratch relies on shortcuts \citep{lin2025}. In standard models the first hop of a two-hop question is often resolved latently, the full chain less reliably \citep{yang2024latently,biran2024hopping}; last-token probes rarely detect intermediate steps in prompted implicit reasoning \citep{yu2024}, and sub-answers are computed while the chain is written \citep{kudo2026}.

\textbf{Probing and its limits.} We build on control tasks and selectivity \citep{hewitt2019}, conditional probing \citep{hewitt2021conditional} and difference-in-means probing \citep{marks2023}; our learning curves are close in spirit to information-theoretic and minimum-description-length probing \citep{voita2020mdl,pimentel2020information}. \citet{park2024} define concept directions from counterfactual pairs, as our minimal pairs do. Probing critiques \citep{belinkov2022probing} stress that a readable property need not be used \citep{ravichander2021probing,elazar2021amnesic}; a probe can also fail because of how its target is framed \citep{nanda2023emergent}. Interchange interventions \citep{geiger2021causal,geiger2024finding} and causal mediation with counterfactual inputs \citep{vig2020investigating,stolfo2023,hanna2023greaterthan} test whether a variable is used; subspace patches can mislead by acting through dormant directions \citep{makelov2023}, and the copy projection guards against a related risk. \citet{ye2024} read needed quantities at the question's end in models trained from scratch. To our knowledge, no earlier work reads a copied token at a summary position with minimal pairs while a standard probe fails, or tests a computed value against copies, an operation swap and calibrated mixtures.

\textbf{Number representations.} Numbers are encoded partly linearly \citep{zhuf2024} and partly by periodic features with periods such as 2, 5 and 10 \citep{zhou2024fourier,kantamneni2025trigonometry,nanda2023progress}. Arithmetic can come from a bag of heuristics \citep{nikankin2025arithmetic}, which fits the generic mixing we see; small transformers trained on addition build carry features \citep{quirke2024addition}.

\section*{Impact Statement}

This paper presents work whose goal is to advance the field of machine learning, specifically methods for reading what language models compute internally. Such methods are used to monitor models, and the paper's main point is a caution for that use: a failed probe does not show that a value is absent, and a successful one does not show that it was computed or used. We see no other societal consequences that need discussion here.

\section*{Reproducibility}

Code, pre-registrations, frozen file hashes, analysis outputs and decision rules for every experiment are in a public repository (\url{https://github.com/Aur-Shalev-Merin/present-but-hard-to-read}), including per-item results for the post-hoc comparisons. Every pre-registration was committed, and pushed to a private development repository, before its run or analysis; the public repository lists each freeze commit, when it was pushed, and the time of the first run. Each experiment's document records its predictions, its results, and every change made before data.

\section{The copied digit in more detail}\label{app:presence}

\subsection{Minimal pairs in Ouro}\label{sec:mp}

A standard probe at the last prompt token does not read a digit copied from the question. In a pre-registered study of intermediate values, its sanity check, the last digit of the largest number in the question, scored 40.8\%, exactly the rate of always guessing the most common digit. By position, the probe reads a copied digit perfectly on its own token, about half the time one token later (early in the first loop only), and at chance from three tokens on, consistent with the rapid loss of earlier-token information found at the last token of multi-token words in other models \citep{feucht2024}. In bare questions such as ``What is 247 plus 580?'' the same probe reads both operands at the last token at 0.96--1.00, but those prompts differ only in their numbers, which makes them close to minimal pairs.

We took one question per template and one of its multi-digit numbers, and made ten copies that differ only in that number's last digit (0--9): 137 such families from 77 distinct questions, one per template (a template's two families vary different numbers of the same question). The copies are identical token for token except one, so any difference in a later state is caused by that digit. Counterfactual minimal pairs are a standard interpretability control \citep{stolfo2023,vig2020investigating}; subtracting a family's mean removes what the family shares, the contrast that difference-in-means probing uses to find a direction \citep{marks2023}. We then repeated the test with an added sentence that cannot matter (``Unrelated fact: a nearby park has 43 benches.''), varying either its number or a colour word (``43 red benches''). Table~\ref{tab:mp} and Figure~\ref{fig:readout} give the results in Ouro.

Every varied token reaches the position where the answer begins, and the minimal-pair probe reads it on templates it never saw. Any changed input leaves some change in later states; what matters is that this one is in a code shared across questions, which a probe trained on other templates reads. The code is shared: a probe trained on needed numbers reads irrelevant ones at 0.78--0.97. Nor does the readout need the whole family: applied to each test prompt minus a single other variant of its question, the same probe reads the needed digit at 0.79--0.84 (two variants, 0.87--0.91; post hoc). The irrelevant number almost never changes the first token of Ouro's answer (at most 2.6\% of families), so the trace is the copied token, not the answer in progress; this fits GSM-NoOp, where an irrelevant clause lowers accuracy \citep{mirzadeh2024}, although we did not test whether the trace causes errors. The code is not a magnitude direction \citep{zhuf2024}, consistent with per-digit codes \citep{levy2025}: a linear value component exists ($R^2$ 0.57), but the probe's errors are no closer to the true digit than random guesses (mean distance 3.8 against 3.7), and the top one to four principal components read the digit at only 0.21--0.45, against 0.93--0.97 with 256. It behaves like a code for which token was there, as the colour result suggests. A shared code is what copying predicts, since an attention head that moves a token writes roughly the same vector for it whatever the context; binding vectors that transfer across tasks have been found the same way \citep{feng2024binding}. The minimal-pair probe needs all ten variants of a question, so it is a test for presence, not a decoder. One sibling does not replace the family: in a pre-registered follow-up, subtracting a single variant with a fixed other digit instead of the family mean reads 0.65--0.90, against 0.79--0.96 with all ten, in all four models of this paper; the gap is largest where the signal is weakest, and it depends on which digit serves as the reference.

\subsection{A small change, not a crowded one}\label{sec:swamp}

A trace at a few percent of the variance would still be easy to read if questions hardly varied along its directions. They vary a lot. Take the digit's directions to be the 9-dimensional span of the family-centred digit means, and its \emph{overlap} to be the context's variance per dimension inside that span over its average per dimension. The overlap is 6.5--11.7 in Ouro and 15--24 in Qwen, so within its own subspace the digit's signal is at most a few percent of the variance around it (Ouro by state in Appendix~\ref{app:state}). The overlap is not specific to the digit. A subspace fitted with the digit labels shuffled within each family, which carries no shared code, has about the same (ratio 0.83 in Ouro and 1.01 in Qwen), and a colour word and a number varied in the added sentence are each about as crowded as their own shuffled-label baselines, or less. Any change within a question lands on the directions along which questions vary most.

What differs between changes is their size (Table~\ref{tab:perturb}). In Qwen we also replaced the digit with one of ten random words, or added one of ten random vectors of a typical token embedding's size to its embedding. All three changes land on equally busy directions: by the pre-registered measure, the word swap's overlap is 0.89 times the digit's and the noise's 1.07. Their signal-to-noise inside their own subspaces differs by orders of magnitude, and readability follows it: a standard probe reads the word swap at 0.24--0.74, and the digit and the noise at 0.11--0.15. The input embeddings show where part of the difference starts. In both models the ten digits sit about half as far apart as random words do (0.70 against 1.37 for 60 random words in Qwen, 0.88 against 1.54 in Ouro; the ten swap words of Table~\ref{tab:perturb} are 1.42 apart). But the start does not explain all of it: the word and noise variants start equally far apart, and only the word change reaches the last token, while the noise arrives as small as the digit or smaller. These comparisons are descriptive. A pre-registered follow-up measured the linear response of the last token to small changes at the digit's input embedding (Appendix~\ref{app:state}). To first order, the last token is about as sensitive to random input directions as to directions in which token embeddings differ (gain ratio 1.2--1.5), and the word swap's effect is almost entirely nonlinear: its linear prediction is orthogonal to the actual change and 4.5--15 times too small. What reaches the last token depends on which token is present, not on how far the input moves. Small changes along digit directions are even carried more strongly than along word directions (3--8 times, to first order), so the digit's small trace goes with swapping one digit for another, a smaller change in kind than swapping in a word. Which mechanism carries a token's identity to the last token is open.

The same null, pre-registered for the larger models, gives the same picture in Qwen2.5-7B (word swap 0.90 and noise 0.84 of the digit's overlap; the word swap reads at 0.27--0.93, the digit at 0.13--0.27 and the noise at 0.10--0.12). In Ouro-2.6B it is mixed: the noise is as crowded as the digit (1.28 times its overlap) and as unreadable, but the word swap lands on quieter directions (0.34 times) and reads at 0.82--0.86. There, both size and crowding favour the word, and ``small, not crowded'' describes the digit against noise only.

This is not superposition in the usual sense \citep{elhage2022}: the digit shares its directions with the variation between contexts, not with a fixed set of other features. Probing work usually asks whether a probe's success reflects the representation or the probe's capacity \citep{hewitt2019}; here a probe fails because a signal shared across contexts is small next to the variation the contexts add along the same directions, and only the minimal-pair contrast removes that variation. The same limit is known from population coding in neuroscience, where only noise along the signal's own direction limits a decoder \citep{morenobote2014}.

\textbf{Linear response at the varied token.} A pre-registered study took 24 of the questions, one per template, with their original digit and the same prompt, and measured by finite differences how the last prompt token's state responds to small changes of the digit's input embedding (step 0.02 times the median embedding norm). The squared gain along directions in which word embeddings differ is 1.16--1.28 times that along random directions in Qwen (layers 7--28) and 1.36--1.50 in Ouro (loop ends), against the 3 that would have counted as tuned to token directions; we had predicted tuned. Along digit-difference directions it is 2.7--8.5 and 4.4--5.0 times the word-direction gain. The response is linear only locally: for the full word swap, the linear prediction is orthogonal to the actual change ($\cos \approx 0$) and 4.5--15 times too small, while the full-size noise acts roughly linearly ($\cos$ 0.38--0.68). Per unit input, a word swap changes the last token 10--25 (Qwen) and 38--373 (Ouro) times more in squared size than the noise, and a digit swap 0.12--0.94 and 0.23--0.36 times as much as a digit-to-word swap. Causal tracing corrupts inputs by adding Gaussian noise to token embeddings \citep{meng2022locating}, and \citet{zhang2024patching} recommend replacing tokens instead; here, noise of a token embedding's size barely reaches the summary position, while a token swap does.

A further test, used only in Studies 1 and 2, checks mixtures of the output. On units digits, $a+b$ is the answer plus $c$, so a state that mixes the answer it is about to write with a copy of $c$ could make $a+b$ readable. $a+b$ tops the line answer $+\,k c$ by +0.075 [0.052, 0.098] in Study 2 and +0.048 [0.030, 0.066] in Study 1, but a calibrated null on Study 3's items shows that generic mixing tops such a line too (Section~\ref{sec:early}); $a+b$ also tops the other two-number combinations by 0.09--0.13. Such mixtures do exist: after loop 4, forms on the answer lines read 0.06--0.07 above forms on no answer line. The margin also survives the stricter baselines. Adding the answer digit already written changes nothing, and adding the carry and borrow, which Ouro does not see as tokens, lowers the Study 2 margin to +0.048 [0.023, 0.074]. On problems Ouro answers correctly it is +0.064 [0.037, 0.091], and resampling templates instead of families does not widen the loop-4 intervals.

No probe label can separate ``$a+b$ was formed and the answer computed from it'' from ``the answer was formed and $c$ added back'': they are the same function of the digits. The timing does not help the first reading: after loop 1, $a+b$ and the answer digit read about equally (+0.16 each in Study 2), and after loop 2 the answer reads higher (+0.53 against +0.38). At the moment it is needed, then, $a+b$ is readable beyond what copies allow and beyond its sign-flipped twin, in two studies with different templates, and it tops its answer line by about as much as generic mixing does. Two questions remain. Every problem needed $a+b$, so a fixed preference for sums at P1 would also explain the sign-flip result. And whether Ouro uses the value needs an intervention. Study 3 addresses both.

\subsection{Study 3: swapping the operation, and patching}\label{sec:study3}

Study 3 was pre-registered in two parts: Part A, the operation swap, and Part B, patching. Its 12 new templates come in two versions that differ only in the first step's phrase: ``gave her $B$ more stickers'' gives $a+b-c$, and ``took $B$ of her stickers'' gives $a-b-c$. A plus family and its minus twin share names, tens digits and unit-digit combinations. Each version has 7,200 problems, and probes are trained and scored within a version. Let $D_+$ be the excess of $a+b$ minus that of $a-b$ on plus problems, $D_-$ the reverse on minus problems, and $I = (D_+ + D_-)/2$. A fixed preference for either sign adds the same amount to one $D$ and takes it from the other, so it cancels in $I$; we report it as $F = (D_- - D_+)/2$. The label FOLLOWS required $I \geq 0.05$ at P1 after loop 4, with its interval above zero and both $D$s positive.

All gates passed: accuracy after loop 4 was 0.67 on plus and 0.77 on minus problems, the copies at P0 read at 0.98--1.00 at their best state, and the bare sum digit at 1.00. The label is FOLLOWS (Table~\ref{tab:study3}): $I = +0.157$ [0.141, 0.172], with 97.5\% intervals that resample twin families together. On minus problems $a-b$ reads +0.27 and $a+b$ +0.08, nearly the mirror image of plus problems. The fixed preference is small and favours differences, $F = +0.032$ [0.012, 0.053], as bare addition suggested. The digit Ouro is about to write depends on the carry or borrow from the units, which is sign-specific, so a pre-registered stricter check adds the carry and borrow to the copy ceiling. With them, $I$ at P1 is +0.126 [0.107, 0.145], still well above the bar, and at P0, before the answer starts, it is $-0.013$ [$-0.028$, 0.002]: the accuracy swap finds nothing sign-specific there beyond the carry and borrow, and the share swap is UNCLEAR there. The copy of $b$ reads only 0.77--0.81 there after loop 4 (0.66 in Study 1), so P0 is the weaker test. In both versions the needed form also tops the other forms on its answer line after loop 4, by +0.036 and +0.045, which generic mixing matches (Section~\ref{sec:early}). So the sign-flip result of Studies 1 and 2 is not a preference for sums at P1: the better-read sign is the one the problem needs. This still does not separate a first step formed before the answer from the answer with $c$ added back, since in both versions the needed form is the answer plus $c$. These are the accuracy-based swap's numbers; the share swap's re-score of P1 is in Section~\ref{sec:ouro} (FOLLOWS, $I/N = 0.63$, and 0.57 with the carry and borrow regressed, against a bar of 0.25 set on this design).

Part B asks whether Ouro uses the value. For two plus problems $x$ and $x'$ from the same family, we run both to a patch point, replace $x$'s component in a 9-dimensional subspace $U$ at P1 with $x'$'s, $h \leftarrow h + UU^\top(h_{x'} - h)$, run the remaining loops, and read the digit predicted at P1. \subsection{Patching before the answer forms, and a null for mixing}\label{sec:early}

Two further pre-registered studies address the weakest points above. The first repeats the patching on the same pairs in three ways that leave no formed answer to override $a+b$ (Table~\ref{tab:early}). A single patch at the end of loop 1, where $a+b$ and the answer read about equally, cannot be scored: loops 2--4 undo any patch made there, even one of the answer (hit 0.009). Clamping, which sets the component to the partner's at the end of loops 1, 2 and 3, does not have that problem: clamping the answer moves the written digit in 91\% of pairs, while clamping $a+b$ moves it to the partner's digit in about 4\% of pairs, below the $a-b$ control and above the random one (0.3\%), and Ouro keeps its own digit in 90--97\% of pairs. Both clamp tests are NOT-USED, including the equal-answer pairs, in which an $a+b$ patch cannot carry the partner's answer. In the third test the answer's component is first set to its family mean at the end of loop 3. Ouro then writes its own digit in only 31--32\% of pairs, so the $a+b$ component left at P1 does not rebuild the answer, and the partner's does not steer the digit: every condition, random included, hits the target at about chance (0.08--0.10). This test is weaker than the clamp. It has no positive control, and with the answer removed the output is largely scrambled, so a null there may reflect a disrupted state rather than an unused value.

The second study calibrates the protocol's mixture tests. We built synthetic states on Study 3's items with copies of $a$, $b$ and $c$, a strong answer code and no $a\pm b$ term, plus one generic mixture of the four digits: products of random codes for every pair, or same-frequency products of periodic digit features, or a random one-layer network. The mixing strength was set so that the needed form reads about as well as in Ouro, and the paper's own probes then scored each world. Pairwise mixing tops the answer line by as much as Ouro does or more (mean margins +0.078 and +0.051 over six worlds each, against +0.036 and +0.045), so by the pre-registered rule the line test does not separate a held $a\pm b$ from mixing. The reason is that the needed form is a function of two inputs, $a$ and $b$, which pairwise mixing makes readable directly, while the other forms on its line need all three; in the Fourier worlds, answer $\pm 2c$ barely reads, as same-frequency products predict. The random network never made the needed form as readable as in Ouro, so it could not be calibrated. The same worlds give an operation-swap statistic $I$ of +0.09 on average and at most +0.14, against +0.157 in Ouro: the sign-following signal exceeds every null world, but mixing alone produces about half of it. In small transformers trained from scratch on the same task, dense checkpoints did not separate the two accounts either: the sign-specific signal never appeared while the answer was absent, and two of three architectures solved the two-digit task with no readable first partial result (Appendix~\ref{sec:v220}). These comparisons use the accuracy-based swap; the share swap's bar comes from 420 calibration worlds instead, and 420 fresh ones gave no false positive (Section~\ref{sec:control}); 270 worlds rebuilt on Study 3's design set Ouro's bar at 0.25 (Section~\ref{sec:ouro}).

\subsection{Loop dynamics}\label{sec:dynamics}

At P1, every combination of the numbers we checked reads better at the end of a loop than in its middle, including $a+c$, which the problem never needs, while the copy of $b$ reads better in the middle. This is not an effect of normalization: Ouro normalizes the state it feeds into the next loop, but dividing each mid-loop state by its own size leaves the pattern unchanged. Within each loop, the second half of the layers makes combinations of the numbers readable. Unneeded ones then fade: $a+c$ peaks after loop 2 (+0.46) and falls to +0.09 by loop 4, while $a+b$ peaks after loop 3 (+0.45) and keeps +0.22. In Study 3 the needed form peaks after loop 3 on plus problems (+0.51) and after loop 2 on minus problems (+0.42).

\subsection{Training order in small models}\label{sec:v220}

A pre-registered study asked whether a sign-specific $a \pm b$ appears before the answer during training, which would favour a held partial result over mixing. Eighteen small transformers were trained from scratch on answer-only $a \pm b - c$: a 6-layer model, a 2-layer block looped three times, and a 2-layer model of the looped one's size, on two-digit numbers and on arithmetic mod 23, three seeds each. Probes with copy ceilings and the operation swap ran every 100 (two-digit) or 50 (mod) training steps. No run showed the signal while the answer was absent. On two-digit numbers, the looped and the size-matched models reach about 99\% accuracy with no sign-specific $a \pm b$ at any position or checkpoint ($I \le 0.034$), and the 6-layer model shows it only after the answer in two of three seeds. On mod 23, all three architectures show it with or after the answer. On a fixed bar of 0.05 the signal crosses later than the answer, but much of that is a threshold effect: the answer signal peaks near 0.97 and $I$ at 0.06--0.26, and measured against each signal's own peak, the two rise within one to three checkpoints of each other in mod 23. Looping made no difference on mod 23; on two-digit numbers the comparison is undefined, since neither the looped nor the size-matched model showed the signal. These checks use the accuracy-based swap.

\section{The re-test of \citet{kudo2026} in detail}\label{app:kudo}

\citet{kudo2026} conclude that language models ``have not derived an answer at the moment when input was fed into the model'' and compute (sub-)answers while writing the chain of thought. Their evidence is a standard probe for each token position and layer, which before the chain reads the sub-answer at 50.5\% and the answer at 17.9\%, against 95--100\% inside it and a 90\% threshold, and patching, in which the input part barely affects the chain. We applied the protocol to their task in a pre-registered study: their two-step problems ($a = k \pm b$, $b = d_1 \pm d_2$, then ``$a$?''), in their format with three worked examples, in Qwen2.5-7B, which writes their chain exactly in 99.8\% of problems. We read the states at two positions before the chain, the end of $b$'s equation and the last prompt token. A family varies $d_1$ and $d_2$ over every valid pair, in a plus and a minus version; the constant $k$ varies across families, which separates $b$ from the answer. We also ran their short chain format (``$b = 5$, $a = 6$''), in which the chain does not restate $b$'s equation.

Their probe replicates: on single prompts it reads $b$ before the chain at 0.64 (their 50.5\%) and $a$ at 0.32 (their 17.9\%). The protocol shows what that readability is (Table~\ref{tab:kudo}). At both positions and in both formats the operands are combined beyond the copy ceiling: $(d_1+d_2) \bmod 5$ and $(d_1-d_2) \bmod 5$ read 0.12--0.38 above it. The original accuracy-based swap called neither position FOLLOWS ($I = 0.00$ and $+0.03$ against its bar of 0.05), but that swap later failed its positive control (Section~\ref{sec:control}). Re-scored with the validated share swap (Table~\ref{tab:kudo}), both positions are UNCLEAR: the state prefers the difference on both kinds of problem ($D_+ < 0$), with a small part that follows the operation ($I = +0.020$ and $+0.010$, intervals clear of zero). By the pre-registered rule, nothing places $b$ before the chain. Nor does the chain use such a value. In their format, replacing the whole state at either position with another problem's, from layer 12 on, never changed the $b$ the chain writes in a 40-pair check before the freeze (0 of 40; the main format's patch was not run as a pre-registered test): the chain restates $b$'s equation and computes from that text. In the short format, replacing the state at the end of $b$'s equation changes the written $b$ to the other problem's in 96\% of pairs, and so does replacing only the directions of the copied digit (96\%); the directions of $b$ with the copy projected out do so in 2\%, and the last prompt token feeds that step in none.

Nothing in the protocol's results places the sub-answer before the chain, and the chain computes it from the operands, but the swap cannot tell at these positions. They also show how two weaker tests would have disagreed (their probe, at 0.64, is below their 90\% threshold): the operands are combined well beyond the copy ceiling, and clamping $b$'s subspace without removing the copied digit moves the written $b$ in 30\% of pairs. Both suggest an early sub-answer. The share swap does not find a code that follows the operation (UNCLEAR), and the copy-controlled clamp finds $b$'s own directions barely move the written $b$, and it keeps 75--85\% of them, more than a clamp of this form kept when it detected a used value in small models (post hoc; Appendix~\ref{app:v230}). Their probe also misreads a value that is certainly present (post hoc, descriptive): on the same replication states it reads the copied operand $d_1$ at 0.59 at the last prompt token, below their 90\% threshold, though minimal pairs read it at 1.00 there. It reads the other operand, $d_2$, at 0.998 at the same token, and both at 1.00 at the end of $b$'s equation, so its rule misses one of the two operands, and only at the end of the question.

\paragraph{Without a chain of thought.} \citet{kudo2026} also have the model answer directly (``$a = 6$''). Their probe then reads $b$ at 69\% and $a$ at 41\% before the answer, both below their threshold, and they conclude that when the model fails, the sub-answers are unlikely to be represented in its states. Their probe replicates here as well: run as in the chain study (post hoc), it reads $b$ at 0.74 at the token before the answer digit (0.63 within the prompt) and $a$ at 0.32 within the prompt. In this setting the model has to form $b$, or find a shortcut, without writing it. Qwen2.5-7B answers 69\% of random problems like theirs directly (12,000 problems; their 77.8\%; our pilot, 67.5\%). The gap of about 9 points may reflect differences between our re-implementation of their format and theirs. On our families it answers 76\%: 88\% when $b$'s operation is a plus and 64\% when it is a minus, falling to 38\% when both operations subtract. In a second pre-registered study we read the same positions plus a third, the token just before the answer digit. There the answer is itself a confound, since within a family it fixes $b$. The study therefore also clamps the answer's directions, projects them out of $b$'s along with the copy's, and calibrates a null that mixes the copies with the answer and has no $b$ in it. The operands are again combined beyond the copy ceiling at every position (+0.14 to +0.38), but under the original accuracy-based swap no position followed the operation ($I$ from 0.00 to +0.04, against its bar of 0.05), and mixtures of the copies with the answer, with no $b$ in them, gave as much (up to +0.06). Re-scored with the share swap and the answer regressed out, every position is UNCLEAR (Table~\ref{tab:kudo}). The clamp gives the same picture. Replacing the whole state with another problem's changes the answer to that problem's in 85\% of pairs at the two scorable positions. At the end of $b$'s equation the copied digit's directions alone do this in 83\% of pairs. Just before the answer, the answer's own directions do it in 46\% and the copy's in 24\%. $b$'s directions with both the copy and the answer projected out do it in 1\% and 3\%; without the projection, $b$'s directions do it in 28\% and 35\%. Without a chain, then, $b$'s directions beyond the copies and the answer barely move the answer, but with 35--54\% of them kept that test has little shown power (Appendix~\ref{app:v230}); and since the answer fixes $b$ within a family, the design cannot say whether $b$ is held inside the answer's code. Here too the protocol cannot tell. Their sentence about failures is not contradicted either. Split by whether the model answers correctly, both our operation signal and their probe at first point the other way: on wrongly answered problems $I$ is larger, and their probe reads $b$ at 0.77 against 0.73. But two thirds of the wrong answers come from problems in which both operations subtract, where $b$ reads best. Compared within each pair of operations (post hoc, rule recorded before running), their probe reads $b$ slightly worse on failures ($-0.04$, 95\% CI $[-0.09, +0.01]$).

\section{An unwritten value at a distance in detail}\label{app:v230}

\paragraph{Task and models.} Prompts are ``[pad] $x \circ z$ ; [$k$ fillers] $w$ ='' with $x, z, w \in \{0, \dots, 22\}$, $\circ \in \{+, -\}$, $k = 6$ or $0$ (pads keep the length fixed), and answer $y = (T[(x \circ z) \bmod 23] + w) \bmod 23$ for a fixed permutation $T$. The intermediate $s = (x \circ z) \bmod 23$ is never written, and since $w$ varies the answer does not fix it. The models are 6-layer transformers ($d = 192$) trained from scratch; five seeds reach 0.995--1.000 held-out accuracy, and each run reproduced, bit for bit, the checkpoint the ground truth was measured on (checked by hash). Negative controls (five seeds, 0.983--0.997) are trained on $y = (T_1[x] + T_2[z] \circ w) \bmod 23$: the same tokens, but $x \circ z$ is never needed.

\paragraph{Ground truth.} In pilots, before any protocol statistic, distributed alignment search \citep{geiger2024finding} learned a subspace at each position and layer and swapped it between two prompts. When the swap carries $s$, the model writes $T[s'] + w$ in 0.78--0.98 of held-out pairs at ``;'' (layers 3--5) in every model, and in at most 0.07 elsewhere, except 0.13--0.18 at ``='' in one model, which is not scored there. Swapping a single operand at ``;'' succeeds in at most 0.08 of pairs, so ``;'' holds $s$ itself, not copies of the operands for later use. At layer 0, where the operands are still on their own tokens, the same search swaps each of them in 0.99--1.00 of pairs. The pre-registered rule: a position is positive if $s$ reaches 0.70 with each operand at most 0.15, negative if $s$ stays at most 0.15.

\paragraph{The protocol.} The share swap is the one used for Ouro and Kudo (copies $x$, $z$, $w$ and the answer regressed out; layers 1--5), with its bar recalibrated on this design from 270 null worlds as in Section~\ref{sec:protocol} (0.35). The clamp follows Section~\ref{sec:protocol} on 1,000 pairs that differ only in $z$ (so in $s$ and the answer, not in $x$ or $w$), with $s$'s subspace projected off $z$'s copy. It differs in how the codes are found. In a check on a known answer, before the freeze ($w$'s own code at $w$'s token), class means of $z$ carried part of $w$'s code, because $w$ is drawn for each pair, and replacing $z$'s subspace moved $w$ in 40--97\% of pairs; the class codes are therefore fitted jointly, by least squares on one-hot codes of $s$, $x$, $z$, $w$ and the operation, and each subspace keeps only the directions above a shuffled-label null. On that check the clamp then gives $+0.86$ and $+0.99$ (one model unscorable).

\paragraph{Results.} At ``;'' the clamp gives $+0.90$, $+0.67$, $+0.60$, $+0.93$, $+0.97$ over the random subspace at $k = 6$ and $+0.85$, $+0.90$, $+0.62$, $+0.91$, $+0.98$ at $k = 0$, every interval's lower end at least $+0.54$; the whole state moves the answer in 0.82--0.99 of pairs and the random subspace in at most 0.003. It is USED nowhere else ($w$'s token at most $+0.09$) and in no negative control. The share swap gives $I/N$ 0.78--0.91 at ``;'' and is FOLLOWS at all 40 scored cells of the five models, fillers, $w$'s token and ``='' included, and at none in the negative controls (largest $I/N$ 0.04). One of 270 fresh null worlds is FOLLOWS: a periodic mixture at the weakest mixing strength, $I/N = 0.363$ against 0.35, with a needed share $N = 0.0009$, about fifty times below that of any cell in the trained models. A floor on $N$ would have excluded it, but none was pre-registered, so the swap is not specific by its rule.

\paragraph{Does the earlier clamp transfer?} The clamps in Sections~\ref{sec:ouro} and~\ref{sec:kudo} used separate class means. They agree with joint codes when the other inputs are balanced within each class; none of the item sets is exactly balanced (Ouro's by sampling, Kudo's by construction, since $b$ is a function of $d_1$, $d_2$ and the operation). Run on the same five models and pairs with separate class means (post hoc), the clamp is USED at ``;'' in all five at both distances ($+0.49$ to $+0.89$ with the copy projected out, $+0.75$ to $+0.93$ without it, the form used for Ouro), and USED at no position where $s$ is absent, though also at ``='' in the one model where the search found $s$ weakly there ($+0.21$ and $+0.24$; joint codes $+0.08$ and $+0.09$). There the class means of $z$'s copy also carry part of $s$'s code, which costs the projected clamp some power.

\paragraph{Is $s$ present where the share swap fires?} The swap's FOLLOWS at the fillers, $w$ and ``='', where DAS and the clamp find no use, could be false positives or could mark a value that is present. An independent check (post hoc) decodes $s$ with a ridge classifier on held-out families, from family-demeaned states with one-hot copies of $x$, $z$, $w$, the operation and the answer regressed out on the training folds (chance 0.04). At the fillers, before $w$ is seen, $s$ reads at 0.90--1.00 in four models and 0.63--0.65 in the fifth, against 0.20--0.31 for random functions of $(x, z)$; in the negative models $s$ reads no better than random functions anywhere (0.07--0.28 against 0.08--0.30). At $w$ and ``='', $s$ reads at 0.85--1.00 in four models, but there $s$ is a function of the answer and $w$, which regressing their one-hot copies does not remove, so these positions are weaker evidence. The value is carried to the fillers and is readable there, but not used: the swap's FOLLOWS there marks presence. The 1 null world in 270 is a true false positive.

\paragraph{Power at a faint signal.} In the same five models a further pre-registered study left the forward pass untouched and degraded only the states the clamp's subspaces are fitted on, adding variance drawn once per item until $s$'s share of the remaining variance (the share swap's $N$, as a population estimate) fell from 0.32--0.47 to 0.1, 0.03, 0.01, 0.005 and 0.002; Ouro's $a+b$ is about 0.008--0.010 on the same scale. The added variance had the covariance of the model's own residual (which crowds $s$'s span at 2.5--5.5 times the average, against 6.5--11.7 for Ouro's context in the digit's span) or was isotropic, and the subspaces were fitted on all of the fitting half or on 34 items per class, Ouro's ratio of items to dimensions. With separate class means and no copy projection, the form used for Ouro, and $s$'s subspace scored against a random one, the clamp is USED in 5 of 5 models at every dose down to 0.005 in Ouro's regime ($+0.73$ to $+0.90$; isotropic, $+0.40$ to $+0.60$), and in 3 of 5 at 0.002 (the other two voided by a calibration miss). Ouro's own comparison, against the subspace of the other sign, is weak here at any dose ($+0.00$ to $+0.15$ with the model's own covariance), because that subspace moves the answer about as often. The joint-code form of the main test keeps 0--1 directions once the signal is faint or the sample Ouro-sized, and then has no effect. The study degrades the measurement, not the model: a value the model itself codes faintly was not tested.

\paragraph{Power of the copy-projected clamp.} The form used for \citet{kudo2026} also projects $b$'s subspace off the class means of the copy, and here the class means of the operands carry much of $s$'s code. A further pre-registered study repeated the degraded-fitting design with this projection, off the class means of $z$ (the procedure used for Kudo, which keeps 55--67\% of $s$'s directions) or of $z$ and $x$ together (the primary arm, since on the one model checked before the freeze it kept 51\%, inside the 35--64\% then reported for the Kudo clamps; on all five, 26--44\%), with 34 items per class, Kudo's ratio of items to dimensions. Projected off $z$, the clamp is USED in 5 of 5 models at every dose down to 0.005 ($+0.23$ to $+0.78$). Projected off $z$ and $x$, it is USED in only 3 of 5 even at full strength ($+0.26$, $+0.10$, $+0.12$); in a fourth it is NOT-USED by the rule ($-0.04$ [$-0.06$, $-0.02$]), though the clamp without the projection moves the answer in 97\% of pairs there, and at 0.005 it is USED in none. By the pre-registered reading the clamp of the Kudo re-test has no shown power at its signal level (UNINFORMATIVE): a projection that keeps about a third of a value's directions can call a used value NOT-USED. A later check (post hoc) found that the range 35--64\% mixed two definitions of the share kept (the chain study averaged over the copy's four principal angles, not over $b$'s nine directions); on the definition used here the Kudo clamps keep 75--85\% with a chain and 35--54\% without. A projection off a random subspace of the same size keeps 99.6--99.9\% of $b$, so the loss reflects $b$'s code lying in the copy's and the answer's directions. The no-chain clamps therefore sit where the primary arm had no power, and the chain clamps keep more of $b$ than the secondary arm, which had power down to 0.005.

\paragraph{Does a computed value show the probe gap?} The gap between a standard probe and minimal pairs (Section~\ref{sec:presence}) was shown for copied digits. In the same five models (post hoc), with new families that fix $x$, $w$ and the operation and vary $z$ over all 23 values, the standard probe reads $s$ at ``;'' (layer 4) at 0.99--1.00, as well as minimal pairs do, and at the middle filler at 0.86--0.99 against 0.94--0.99. These prompts have no varied context, so there is nothing to swamp a small change; the result says that the gap is not specific to copies, not what a computed value in a language model would show.
